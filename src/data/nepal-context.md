## 1. Federal Structure

### Provinces

Nepal adopted a federal system under the Constitution of Nepal 2015 (2072 BS). The country is divided into 7 provinces:

| Province | Capital | Population (2021 Census) | Health-Relevant Notes |
|----------|---------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Koshi Province | Biratnagar | ~4.97 million | BPKIHS (tertiary teaching hospital) in Dharan. Mix of Terai and Hill districts. High malaria-risk areas in eastern Terai. |
| Madhesh Province | Janakpur | ~6.13 million | Most densely populated province. Highest fertility rate. Lowest health facility density per capita. Predominantly Terai. High prevalence of diabetes and CVD. Significant Madheshi and Muslim populations with distinct health-seeking behaviours. |
| Bagmati Province | Hetauda | ~5.96 million | Includes Kathmandu Valley (national referral hospitals: TU Teaching Hospital, Bir Hospital, Patan Hospital). Highest concentration of health workforce and facilities. Urban health challenges (air pollution, NCDs, mental health). |
| Gandaki Province | Pokhara | ~2.48 million | Mix of Hill and Mountain districts. Strong community health networks. Pokhara has regional hospital and KUSMS-affiliated facilities. Tourism-linked health infrastructure in some areas. Remote Mountain districts (Manang, Mustang) face severe access challenges. |
| Lumbini Province | Deukhuri (Butwal) | ~5.12 million | Mix of Terai and Hill. Bhairahawa has emerging medical education centres. Cross-border health-seeking to India common in Terai districts. |
| Karnali Province | Birendranagar | ~1.69 million | Least populated, most remote. Lowest human development indicators. Highest maternal mortality. Lowest physician density. Karnali Academy of Health Sciences (KAHS) established to address workforce gaps. Food insecurity and malnutrition hotspot. |
| Sudurpashchim Province | Godawari (Dhangadhi) | ~2.71 million | Remote western districts. High poverty rates. Cross-border health-seeking to India. Far-Western University developing health sciences programmes. Historically underserved. |

### Local Governments

- **753 local governments**: 6 metropolitan cities, 11 sub-metropolitan cities, 276 municipalities, 460 rural municipalities (gaunpalika)
- Local governments are constitutionally responsible for basic health services, including primary health care centres, health posts, and community health
- Each local government operates health facilities ranging from community health units to primary health care centres

### Health Devolution Post-2015 Constitution

- The 2015 Constitution assigned health service delivery primarily to local governments (Schedule 8)
- Federal government retains policy, standards, regulation, and specialised/tertiary care
- Provincial governments coordinate between federal and local levels, manage provincial hospitals
- **Three tiers**:
  1. **Federal**: Ministry of Health and Population (MoHP), Department of Health Services (DoHS), national policies, standards, drug regulation, tertiary hospitals
  2. **Provincial**: Provincial Health Directorates, provincial hospitals, coordination and monitoring
  3. **Local**: Primary health care centres, health posts, community health units, birthing centres, FCHVs

### Coordination Challenges

- Unclear division of responsibilities in practice between provincial and local levels
- Federal policies sometimes conflict with local implementation capacity
- Health workforce deployment: local governments hire health workers but face shortages; federal secondment system being phased out
- Supply chain management fragmented across three tiers
- Health information systems (DHIS2) still primarily federally managed, with variable local data quality
- Revenue assignment for health unclear — local governments dependent on federal fiscal transfers
- Provincial health directorates are new institutions still building capacity (established post-2017)

## 2. Health Financing

### Aggregate Spending

- **Total health expenditure (THE) as % of GDP**: ~4.5% (National Health Accounts 2018/19)
- **Government health expenditure as % of THE**: ~26% (low by global standards)
- **Out-of-pocket expenditure (OOP)**: ~51% of THE — among the highest in South Asia
- **Government health spending per capita**: approximately USD 14-16 per capita (varies by year and exchange rate)
- **External funding**: ~12-15% of THE from development partners
- **Catastrophic health expenditure**: ~10-11% of households face catastrophic health spending (>10% of household consumption)
- **Impoverishment due to health spending**: ~1.7% of population pushed below poverty line annually by health costs

### National Health Insurance Programme (NHIP / Social Health Insurance)

- **Legal basis**: Health Insurance Act 2017 (2074 BS); Health Insurance Board (HIB) established 2015
- **Launch**: Pilot began in 3 districts in 2016 (Kailali, Baglung, Ilam); expanded nationally from 2018
- **Coverage as of 2024/25**:
  - Enrolled in all 753 local governments (geographic coverage complete)
  - ~4.3 million enrolled individuals (~14% of population) [needs verification — enrollment figures change rapidly]
  - Target: universal enrollment, but voluntary enrollment model limits uptake
- **Premium**: NPR 3,500 per family of up to 5 members per year (NPR 700 per additional member). Government subsidises premiums for ultra-poor, elderly (>70), and specific vulnerable groups.
- **Benefits package**: Up to NPR 100,000 per family per year. Covers outpatient consultations, inpatient care, emergency services, medicines (from essential medicines list), diagnostics, maternity services, selected surgical procedures. Excludes: cosmetic procedures, overseas treatment, some high-cost specialised care.
- **Key challenges**:
  - Low enrollment and renewal rates (adverse selection — sick people enroll more)
  - Limited provider network quality, especially in rural areas
  - Claims processing delays and provider payment bottlenecks
  - Narrow benefits ceiling (NPR 100,000 insufficient for major illness)
  - Weak IT infrastructure for claims management
  - Public awareness remains low in many districts
  - Sustainability concerns: government subsidies needed to cover deficit

### Aama Programme (Safe Motherhood / Free Maternity Services)

- Free institutional delivery services since 2005 (Aama Suraksha Karyakram)
- Transport incentive: cash payment to mothers who deliver at health facilities (NPR 1,500-3,000 depending on ecological zone)
- Four ANC visits, institutional delivery, and PNC covered
- Credited with significant increase in institutional delivery rates (from ~18% in 2006 to ~77% in 2022)
- Challenges: delayed reimbursement to facilities, quality of care concerns, informal payments persist

### Basic Health Service Package (BHSP)

- Defined by MoHP: set of essential health services that all citizens should access free at point of care
- Includes: essential medicines, maternal and newborn care, child health services, communicable disease treatment, basic NCD screening, family planning, immunisation
- Implementation varies significantly by local government capacity and funding
- Free Essential Health Care programme provides basic services at government health facilities

## 3. Health Workforce

### Density

- **Physician density**: ~0.17 per 1,000 population (WHO recommends minimum 1.0; SDG threshold 4.45 per 1,000 for physicians, nurses, and midwives combined)
- **Nurse/midwife density**: ~0.67 per 1,000 population
- **Combined physician + nurse/midwife**: ~0.84 per 1,000 (well below WHO minimum threshold of 2.3)
- **Pharmacist density**: ~0.10 per 1,000 population [needs verification]
- **Dentist density**: ~0.03 per 1,000 population

### Female Community Health Volunteers (FCHVs)

- **~52,000 FCHVs** across the country, one per ward
- Volunteer (unpaid) cadre established in 1988
- Roles: health education, family planning distribution, community-based management of childhood illness (CB-IMCI), nutrition counselling, referral facilitation, data collection
- Backbone of community health system, especially in rural areas
- Ageing workforce (many FCHVs now in their 50s-60s); recruitment of younger replacements is challenging
- Debate about professionalisation and compensation (currently receive only allowances for meetings and some supplies)
- During COVID-19, played critical role in contact tracing and vaccination mobilisation

### Brain Drain

- Nepal loses an estimated 400-500 doctors per year to emigration (primarily to US, UK, Australia, Japan)
- ~30% of medical graduates leave within 5 years of qualifying [needs verification]
- Nursing outmigration also significant (Gulf states, UK, Australia)
- Compulsory rural service (2 years post-graduation) has low compliance and enforcement
- Push factors: low salaries, poor working conditions, limited career progression, political instability
- Pull factors: higher salaries, better training opportunities, quality of life abroad

### Urban-Rural Maldistribution

- ~80% of doctors concentrated in urban areas (primarily Kathmandu Valley and a few Terai cities)
- Many rural health posts staffed only by health assistants (HA) or auxiliary health workers (AHW)
- Remote districts (Humla, Dolpa, Mugu, Bajhang) may have 1-2 doctors for entire district
- Telemedicine being piloted to address access gaps but limited by internet connectivity

### Key Training Institutions

- **Institute of Medicine (IOM), TU** — Largest medical school, Kathmandu. Affiliated hospitals: TU Teaching Hospital, Maharajgunj
- **BP Koirala Institute of Health Sciences (BPKIHS)** — Dharan, Koshi Province. Autonomous health sciences university
- **Kathmandu University School of Medical Sciences (KUSMS)** — Dhulikhel Hospital. Known for community-based education model
- **Patan Academy of Health Sciences (PAHS)** — Patan Hospital. Focus on producing doctors for rural Nepal
- **National Academy of Medical Sciences (NAMS)** — Bir Hospital. Postgraduate training
- **Karnali Academy of Health Sciences (KAHS)** — Jumla. Established to produce health workers for remote western Nepal
- Multiple private medical colleges (varying quality, regulated by Nepal Medical Council)
- **Council for Technical Education and Vocational Training (CTEVT)** — Trains health assistants, auxiliary nurse midwives, lab technicians

## 4. Key Health Indicators

### Mortality

| Indicator | Value | Source / Year |
|-----------|-------|---------------|
| Life expectancy at birth | 70.8 years (male: 68.9, female: 72.7) | WHO 2023 estimates |
| Maternal mortality ratio (MMR) | 151 per 100,000 live births | NDHS 2022 |
| Neonatal mortality rate | 16 per 1,000 live births | NDHS 2022 |
| Infant mortality rate | 25 per 1,000 live births | NDHS 2022 |
| Under-5 mortality rate | 29 per 1,000 live births | NDHS 2022 |
| Total fertility rate | 2.1 | NDHS 2022 |

Note: MMR varies dramatically by province — Karnali Province estimated >250 per 100,000 vs Bagmati Province ~60 per 100,000. Geographic and caste/ethnic disparities are substantial.

### Nutrition

| Indicator | Value | Source / Year |
|-----------|-------|---------------|
| Stunting (under-5) | 25% | NDHS 2022 |
| Wasting (under-5) | 7% | NDHS 2022 |
| Underweight (under-5) | 19% | NDHS 2022 |
| Anaemia in women (15-49) | ~36% | NDHS 2022 |
| Low birth weight | ~16% | NDHS 2022 |

Stunting has declined significantly (from 57% in 2001 to 25% in 2022) but remains a major concern, with large disparities by wealth quintile and province. Karnali and Madhesh provinces have highest stunting rates.

### Infectious Disease

| Indicator | Value | Source / Year |
|-----------|-------|---------------|
| TB incidence | ~245 per 100,000 | WHO Global TB Report 2024 |
| TB case detection rate | ~72% | NTP Annual Report |
| HIV prevalence (15-49) | ~0.12% | UNAIDS 2023 |
| Malaria | Near elimination (<500 indigenous cases/year) | DoHS Annual Report |
| Dengue | Increasing incidence — 54,000+ cases in 2022 epidemic | EDCD |
| Kala-azar (visceral leishmaniasis) | Targeted for elimination (<1 per 10,000 in endemic districts) | DoHS |

### Immunisation

| Indicator | Value | Source / Year |
|-----------|-------|---------------|
| DPT3 coverage | ~88% | WHO/UNICEF estimates 2023 |
| Measles 1st dose coverage | ~90% | WHO/UNICEF estimates 2023 |
| Fully immunised (12-23 months) | ~76% | NDHS 2022 |

Nepal's Expanded Programme on Immunisation (EPI) is one of the stronger health programmes, but coverage varies by district and has not yet fully recovered from COVID-19 disruptions in some areas.

### Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs)

- NCDs account for ~66% of all deaths in Nepal (WHO NCD Country Profile 2024)
- **Cardiovascular disease (CVD)**: Leading cause of death. Hypertension prevalence ~25-30% in adults
- **Diabetes**: Estimated prevalence ~8-11% in adults (higher in urban areas, Terai)
- **COPD**: Major burden due to indoor air pollution (biomass fuel use), smoking. Nepal has among the highest COPD mortality rates globally.
- **Cancer**: Cervical cancer is leading cancer among women. Lung, stomach, and breast cancer also significant.
- **Chronic kidney disease**: Growing concern, especially in Terai (possible environmental factors)
- **Tobacco use**: ~27% of adults (much higher in men, ~38%)
- **Alcohol use**: Harmful alcohol use prevalent, especially among certain ethnic groups (Tamang, Rai, Limbu, Newar)
- NCD services largely concentrated in urban tertiary hospitals; primary care NCD screening and management is limited but expanding under WHO PEN (Package of Essential NCD Interventions)

### Mental Health

- **Treatment gap**: >90% — vast majority of people with mental health conditions receive no treatment
- **Psychiatrists**: ~0.36 per 100,000 population (approximately 100 psychiatrists for 30 million people)
- **Psychologists**: even fewer, mostly concentrated in Kathmandu
- **Suicide**: A leading cause of death among women of reproductive age. ~16 per 100,000 population [needs verification]
- **Common conditions**: Depression, anxiety, PTSD (earthquake, conflict legacy), alcohol use disorders
- **Policy**: Mental Health Policy 1996 (outdated); Mental Health Act passed in 2006; implementation very limited
- **Stigma**: Severe — mental illness attributed to supernatural causes in many communities
- **Integration into primary care**: Pilot projects (mhGAP implementation) in some districts but not yet scaled nationally

## 5. Key Policies & Programmes

### National Policies

| Policy / Strategy | Year | Key Features |
|-------------------|------|-------------|
| National Health Policy 2019 | 2019 (2076 BS) | Emphasises UHC, federalism, health as fundamental right, multi-sectoral approach |
| Nepal Health Sector Strategy (NHSS) 2016-2021 | 2016 | Four strategic directions: equitable access, quality, health system reform, multi-sectoral approach |
| National Health Policy 2071 (predecessor) | 2014 | First post-conflict comprehensive health policy |
| Second Long-Term Health Plan 1997-2017 | 1997 | [Historical context] Guided health sector during conflict and peace periods |

Note: NHSS successor (post-2021 strategy) has been delayed by political transitions; health sector operating under extensions and interim arrangements [needs verification on current status].

### Major Programmes

- **Free Essential Health Care Programme**: Basic outpatient and inpatient services free at government facilities since 2007. Chronic underfunding and informal payments undermine the "free" promise.
- **Aama Suraksha Karyakram (Safe Motherhood Programme)**: Free institutional delivery, ANC/PNC, transport incentives. See Section 2 for details.
- **National Health Insurance Programme (NHIP)**: Social health insurance. See Section 2 for details.
- **Female Community Health Volunteer (FCHV) Programme**: Community-based health promotion and service delivery since 1988. See Section 3 for details.
- **National Immunisation Programme (EPI)**: Routine immunisation for children and pregnant women. One of the strongest vertical programmes.
- **National TB Control Programme (NTP)**: DOTS-based TB treatment. Free diagnosis and treatment. MDR-TB treatment centres expanding.
- **National HIV/AIDS Control Programme**: Focused on key populations. ART coverage improving.
- **Basic Health Service Package (BHSP)**: Defined set of services every citizen should receive free. See Section 2.
- **Multi-Sector Nutrition Plan (MSNP) II 2018-2022**: Cross-sectoral nutrition approach (health, agriculture, education, WASH).
- **National NCD Action Plan**: Aligned with WHO Global NCD targets. PEN being rolled out at primary care level.
- **Safe Abortion Services**: Legal since 2002 (up to 12 weeks on request, 18 weeks in cases of rape/incest, any time if mother's life at risk). Expanded access but geographic barriers remain.

## 7. Demographics & Geography

### Population

- **Total population**: ~30.0 million (Census 2021: 29,164,578)
- **Population growth rate**: ~0.92% per year (slowing due to declining fertility and outmigration)
- **Urban population**: ~66% (Census 2021, reclassified under new municipality definitions). Note: many "urban" areas remain functionally rural — the 2015 reclassification of local governments inflated the urban figure. Functionally urban population is closer to 20-25%.
- **Median age**: ~24 years. Young population but ageing is beginning.
- **Household size**: Average 4.2 members (Census 2021)

### Ecological Zones

| Zone | % of Land Area | % of Population | Characteristics |
|------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Terai (plains)** | ~17% | ~50% | Flat, fertile, densely populated. Borders India. Highest population density. Malaria, kala-azar, dengue endemic areas. Hottest climate. Highest diabetes/CVD burden. Cross-border health-seeking common. |
| **Hill** | ~65% | ~43% | Middle elevations (500-3,000m). Includes Kathmandu Valley. Most health infrastructure. Varied terrain from subtropical valleys to temperate ridges. Road access expanding but many areas still remote. |
| **Mountain** | ~18% | ~7% | Above 3,000m. Extremely remote. Lowest health indicators. Many areas accessible only by foot or air. Cold-related illness, altitude sickness. Highest poverty rates. Sparse health facilities. |

### Caste/Ethnic Composition and Health Disparities

Nepal has 125+ caste/ethnic groups. Major groups with health-relevant disparities:

- **Brahmin/Chhetri (Hill)**: ~29% of population. Generally better health outcomes, higher health literacy, greater access to services.
- **Janajati (Indigenous nationalities)**: ~36% of population. Diverse group (Tamang, Newar, Rai, Limbu, Gurung, Magar, Tharu, etc.). Health outcomes vary widely. Alcohol-related health issues more prevalent in some groups. Traditional healing practices common.
- **Madheshi caste groups**: ~16%. Terai-based. Lower health facility utilisation. Higher fertility rates. Gender disparities in health access more pronounced. Dowry-related violence.
- **Dalit**: ~13% of population. Worst health outcomes across most indicators. Caste-based discrimination affects health-seeking behaviour and provider interaction. Lowest immunisation coverage, highest child mortality. Dalit women face compounded disadvantage.
- **Muslim**: ~5%. Concentrated in Terai. Lower utilisation of maternal health services. Gender barriers to health access.
- **Other marginalised groups**: Chepang, Raute, and other highly marginalised indigenous groups with the poorest health indicators.

**Key disparity patterns**:
- Under-5 mortality in lowest wealth quintile is 2-3x higher than highest quintile
- Institutional delivery rates range from >90% (Brahmin/Chhetri) to <50% (some Dalit and Janajati subgroups)
- Stunting in lowest wealth quintile (~37%) vs highest (~12%)

### Literacy and Education

- **Overall literacy rate**: ~76% (Census 2021)
- **Male literacy**: ~84%
- **Female literacy**: ~69%
- **Gender gap**: Narrowing among younger cohorts but persists, especially in Terai and Mountain districts
- Health literacy is significantly lower than general literacy — implications for health education and informed consent

### Migration and Remittance Economy

- **Labour migrants abroad**: ~3.5-4 million (primarily in Gulf states, Malaysia, India)
- **Remittances**: ~25-28% of GDP — among highest ratios globally
- **Health implications**:
  - "Left-behind" families: elderly and children without primary caregiver
  - Occupational health risks for migrants (construction, manufacturing)
  - Mental health burden: separation anxiety, family stress
  - Returning migrants: some bring health conditions (occupational injuries, infectious diseases)
  - Remittances finance OOP health spending for families — a coping mechanism that masks system failures
  - Seasonal migration to India (open border): complicates disease surveillance and continuity of care
  - COVID-19 highlighted vulnerability of migrant workers and their families

## 8. Comparator Countries

When contextualising evidence from non-Nepal settings, these comparator countries provide useful reference points:

### India
- **Why useful**: Shared open border, similar cultural context (especially for Terai populations), many shared disease burdens, federal structure, similar health system challenges at larger scale
- **Key comparisons**: Ayushman Bharat (PMJAY) vs NHIP; ASHA workers vs FCHVs; state-level variation in health governance; cross-border health-seeking from Nepal
- **Caveats**: India's scale (1.4 billion) and economic diversity make direct comparison challenging. Indian states vary as much as entire countries.

### Bangladesh
- **Why useful**: Similar income level (GDP per capita), but notably better outcomes in maternal health, family planning, immunisation ("Bangladesh paradox")
- **Key comparisons**: Community health worker models (BRAC Shasthya Shebikas), NGO-driven service delivery, garment industry health programmes, microcredit and health
- **Caveats**: Flatter geography (no mountain access barriers), strong NGO sector (BRAC) with no Nepal equivalent, different governance structure (unitary)

### Sri Lanka
- **Why useful**: South Asian country that achieved strong health outcomes (MMR ~29, life expectancy ~77) at relatively low income levels
- **Key comparisons**: Free universal health system, strong primary care, health workforce retention, maternal health success story
- **Caveats**: Island nation, smaller population (~22 million), unitary state, higher income than Nepal, long history of public health investment

### Thailand
- **Why useful**: Achieved UHC at similar GDP per capita to Nepal's current level (UCS launched 2001 when Thailand's GDP per capita was ~USD 1,800)
- **Key comparisons**: Universal Coverage Scheme design, capitation payment model, village health volunteers (1 million), health technology assessment (HITAP), NCD management in primary care
- **Caveats**: Stronger institutional capacity, more centralised system, higher state capacity, different political economy

### Rwanda
- **Why useful**: Achieved dramatic health improvements in low-income, post-conflict context. Community-based health insurance (Mutuelle de Santé) reached >90% coverage.
- **Key comparisons**: Community health worker programme (45,000 CHWs), CBHI model, performance-based financing, rapid scale-up of health services, post-conflict health system rebuilding
- **Caveats**: Very different governance model (centralised, high state capacity/compliance), smaller population (~13 million), significant donor funding, geographic accessibility less challenging

### Ethiopia
- **Why useful**: Health Extension Programme (HEP) deployed 40,000+ Health Extension Workers — largest community health worker programme, relevant to FCHV reform discussions
- **Key comparisons**: HEP model (salaried HEWs vs volunteer FCHVs), health post infrastructure, primary health care unit design, woreda-level health governance (comparable to local government)
- **Caveats**: Much larger population (~120 million), different disease burden (higher malaria/HIV), ongoing conflict in some regions, different cultural context

## 9. Current Reform Priorities

### NHIP Expansion and Enrollment
- Core challenge: moving from ~14% to universal enrollment with a voluntary model
- Debate on mandatory vs voluntary enrollment
- Provider network expansion and quality improvement needed
- Digital claims system upgrade underway
- Integration with other social protection programmes being explored

### Federalism Implementation in Health
- Clarifying roles and responsibilities across three tiers
- Building provincial health directorate capacity
- Health workforce management transition from federal to local
- Fiscal federalism: ensuring adequate health funding reaches local governments
- Harmonising health information systems across tiers

### NCD Service Package Expansion
- Rolling out WHO PEN at primary health care level
- Training primary care workers in hypertension, diabetes screening and management
- Essential NCD medicines in BHSP (availability still inconsistent)
- Cancer screening (cervical cancer with VIA, breast cancer awareness)
- Tobacco and alcohol control enforcement

### Mental Health Integration into Primary Care
- mhGAP (Mental Health Gap Action Programme) piloting in select districts
- Training primary care workers in depression, psychosis, epilepsy, alcohol use disorder identification and management
- Psychosocial counsellors being deployed (post-earthquake programme expanded)
- Community-based psychosocial support through FCHVs (limited)
- New mental health legislation being discussed [needs verification]

### Digital Health
- **DHIS2**: National health management information system. Rolled out across all districts. Data quality variable but improving.
- **Telemedicine**: Multiple pilots (post-earthquake, COVID-19 accelerated). Challenges: internet connectivity in remote areas, regulation, provider acceptance.
- **Electronic medical records (EMR)**: Being piloted in some hospitals. No national standard yet.
- **mHealth**: SMS-based health messaging programmes, mobile phone-based data collection
- **Digital health strategy**: National Digital Health Strategy being developed under MoHP [needs verification on status]

### Health Workforce Retention in Rural Areas
- Bonding schemes for medical graduates (2-year rural service requirement) — poorly enforced
- Remote area allowances and incentives (insufficient to counteract pull of urban practice)
- KAHS and PAHS models: training doctors in rural settings to increase rural retention
- Task-shifting: expanding scope of health assistants, ANMs, and mid-level providers
- Exploring telemedicine to provide specialist support to rural providers
- Local government hiring authority (post-federalism) — mixed results

## Appendix: Key Data Sources

When citing Nepal health statistics, prefer these authoritative sources:

1. **Nepal Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS)** — Gold standard for population health indicators. Most recent: NDHS 2022. Previous: 2016, 2011, 2006, 2001.
2. **Census of Nepal** — Population, demographics. Most recent: 2021 (2078 BS).
3. **DoHS Annual Report** — Programme coverage, facility data, service statistics. Published annually.
4. **National Health Accounts (NHA)** — Health financing data. Most recent: 2018/19.
5. **WHO Country Profile - Nepal** — Standardised health indicators, NCD profiles.
6. **World Bank Open Data** — Economic indicators, health expenditure, comparative data.
7. **HMIS/DHIS2** — Routine health facility data (variable quality but comprehensive coverage).
8. **Nepal Living Standards Survey (NLSS)** — Household economic data including health expenditure.
9. **Nepal Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS)** — UNICEF-supported household survey.
10. **MoHP publications** — Policies, strategies, programme guidelines.
11. **HIB Annual Reports** — NHIP enrollment, claims, financial data.
